# Copyright

## Intellectual Property

Intellectual property (IP) is a set of legal rights that protect the products of human creativity and ingenuity in the form of inventions, expressions, ideas, symbols, and designs. These rights give the author (or owner, in the case where rights have been transferred) the exclusive right to profit from the use or distribution of the IP. There are several different kinds of IP, including **patents**, **copyright**, **trademarks**, **trade secrets**, and **industrial designs** (among others). Each different form of IP has unique requirements, legal regimes, and durations of protection.

## What is Copyright?

Copyright is a form of IP that protects the creators of literary, musical, and artistic works for a limited term (in the US and Canada the term is the creator's life plus 70 years). The limited term is a balance between allowing the author to profit from their work, and the eventual ability for society to freely use and build upon creative outputs.

Works for which copyright has expired, or for which copyright was never obtained in the first place (e.g., many government documents, or those works authors have designated as copyright-free), are considered to be in the _public domain_. Public domain works are free to be copied, adapted, and published.

The core purpose of copyright is to:

- benefit society by incentivising the creation of new works
- grant creators exclusive rights to benefit financially from their work
- protect the reputation of authors and ensure the integrity of their work
- after an appropriate period, revert the created work to the public domain to enable cultural enrichment and ongoing creation

In most regions of the world, copyright obtains as soon as the author fixes a unique expression in a medium (whether that be written words, a painted image, musical notation, performance, etc.). This is true in the United States, as well, but a quirk of their copyright regime means that authors must register their work with the US Copyright Office to be eligible for statutory damages in copyright infringement litigation.

## Exceptions to Copyright

Copyright protection is not exclusive. The enactors of copyright legislation have created a set of exceptions that protect certain uses of copyrighted works for education and cultural discourse. This allows critics to quote from a text, or educators to show an image or graph from a book, or comedians to satirize a movie, all without fear of a copyright infringement claim. In the US, these exceptions are called _fair use_, and in Canada they are called _fair dealing_. Generally, these exceptions allow for the limited use of copyrighted works for various purposes. In Canada, the allowable purposes are enumerated as:

- Criticism or review
- News reporting
- Research or private study
- Education, and
- Parody

Now, these exceptions are limited, and usually only allow the republication of portions of a protected work. Courts in both countries have shaped the allowable scope of the exempted use in similar ways, with the goal of making the copying _fair_. Generally, the fairness factors include the purpose of the use, whether the use is transformative or not, the amount of copying, whether there were free alternatives, and the effect of the copy on the market and value of the original work.

## Copyright and AI

There are two principal ways in which copyright is implicated in AI:

- **Model training**: To create AI models, vast swaths of copyrighted material have been ingested by companies like OpenAI and Anthropic. Did the authors give permission? Were they compensated for the use of their works? In most cases, no, and numerous court cases are challenging this usage on copyright grounds.
- **AI output**: What is the copyright status of AI-generated work? Does the human directing the AI system own the copyright?

### Model Training

Gen-AI tools are trained by feeding them vast corpora of data in the form of text, images, audio, or other material. Through complex methods (detailed elsewhere on this site), the models "learn" to identify and replicate patterns. In many cases the data used for training is scraped from publicly available sources (e.g., news sources available on the internet, online portfolios, etc.). In some cases, the data has been pirated. Some of this material may be in the public domain (e.g. government data sources, older books), but most is under copyright protection. The creators of these books, images, and music have mostly _not_ been asked for permission, and have not been compensated for the use of their work.

This is especially problematic where the outputs of the Gen-AI models may ultimately threaten the **creative livelihood of the original creators**. In response, several lawsuits have been initiated by groups of artists, authors, and publishers to prevent AI companies from using their work without consolation, consent, and compensation.

The **litigation landscape here is changing rapidly**. Some of these lawsuits have been settled: in September of 2025, the AI giant Anthropic agreed to pay \$1.5 billion USD to litigants (Bartz v. Anthropic) whose books had been pirated for training (Brittain, 2026). By contrast, many AI companies and proponents claim that the unauthorized copying of protected works for AI training falls under the _fair use_ exception (or _fair dealing_ in the Canadian context). This is a very active area of litigation, with few decisions. However, one recent case in the US (Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence) found that copying for AI training did not constitute fair use; it remains to be seen if this is a bellwether decision for other cases.

It is important to note that the legal and regulatory landscape may change. AI proponents are seeking amendments to copyright legislation that may weaken creators' rights and allow for mass training on any publicly accessible data. On the other hand, creators' groups are seeking the right to refuse participation in training, and/or the compensation of creators on a rights-licensing model (similar to existing organizations in many countries that collect and disburse copyright licensing funds).

### Copyright status of AI output

Who owns the output of a generative-AI model? The answer to that question is not simple. In terms of _property rights_, the answer would be: "the prompter or initiator of the AI model." In terms of _copyright_, the answer would in most cases be: "no one;" the work is in the public domain. In Canada, the US, and most of Europe, **_human authorship_ is required** for a creative work to be eligible for copyright protection.

Where there are both human and machine contributions to a work, the human-authored elements can have copyright protection, while the machine-generated elements cannot.

However, in some jurisdictions, such as the UK, copyright legislation is written to expressly allow copyright protection for AI-generated works. AI companies in North America are lobbying for changes to copyright legislation to allow for copyright protection for AI-generated work.

#### COPYRIGHT STATUS OF AI OUTPUT IN CANADA

To be eligible for copyright protection, the Canadian Copyright Act (Department of Justice Canada 2024) requires that a work be:

- original (not a mere copy of another work),
- an "expression of an idea with an exercise of skill and judgment"
- fixed in some medium, and
- authored by a "natural person." (Library of Parliament 2025)

The **copyrightability of AI outputs in Canada is uncertain**, since AI is not currently addressed in copyright legislation, and at present there are no decided court cases to guide legal interpretation. It is thought, though, that the requirement for skill and judgement and a "natural person" author will prevent AI-generated works that have little human input from obtaining copyright protection. Most experts agree that even the writing of extensive and detailed prompts will not be seen as sufficient control for the granting of copyright. Mixed copyright scenarios are anticipated where, for example, human artists provide a sketch for the AI to render: the visible parts of the human-authored sketch will be eligible for copyright, the AI-rendered elements will not.

As an example of current Canadian litigation, in 2021 Ankit Sahni registered a copyright in Canada for an AI-generated image (SURYAST). "The Canadian Intellectual Property Office (CIPO) granted the registration through its automated system, a process involving no substantive review of authorship, ownership, or originality" (Canadian Internet Policy and Public Interest Clinic, n.d.). The grant of copyright is currently being challenged in court by CIPPIC. Notably, the US Copyright Office refused to register SURYAST.

#### COPYRIGHT STATUS OF AI OUTPUT IN THE US

The US Copyright Office has issued a series of reports on AI and copyright (<https://www.copyright.gov/ai/>), which contain useful and approachable discussions of the issues. Their current understanding supports the centrality of human authorship but acknowledges situations where mixed copyright may be appropriate.

In their report on AI Copyrightability (United States Copyright Office 2025): "The Report distinguishes between (1) using AI as a tool to **assist** in creating works (assistive uses, such as de-aging or crowd removal), (2) using AI as a **stand-in** for human creativity, and (3) using AI as a **brainstorming** tool. The Report notes (1) assistive uses and (3) brainstorming uses do not limit copyright protection (i.e., copyright can subsist)." (Yi and Chau)

The US court case US v. Thaler is similar to the SURYAST case in Canada (noted above). The original judgement and initial appeals found that works solely generated by AI are not eligible for copyright (Brittain et al. 2025). In March of 2026, an appeal of this verdict was denied by the US Supreme Court (Jacobs 2026), essentially enshrining the lower court's opinion that "human authorship is a bedrock requirement of copyright."

In a more recent case, the US Copyright Office partially reversed their initial refusal to grant copyright protection to an AI-generated work (Fauchoux and Kahn, 2025). Kent Keirsey, CEO of AI company Invoke AI, had used his software to generate the digital artwork "A Single Piece of American Cheese" with AI. The Copyright Office initially rejected the copyright claim, but after the author provided extensive documentation of the highly iterative creation process, they protected the "selection, coordination, and arrangement of material generated by artificial intelligence." However, they expressly disallowed the protection of "2-D artwork, \[and\] AI-generated image components." Some AI advocates see this as vindication, allowing the copyrighting of AI-generated works, but in truth the result is much murkier: **some parts of a work may be copyrighted, and others not**.
